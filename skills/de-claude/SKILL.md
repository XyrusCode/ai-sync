# de-claude: remove AI fingerprints from a repository

humanizer fixes prose. This fixes everything a repo leaks *besides* prose: trailers, authorship, history, branch names, config files, and comments that narrate a diff. When a commit message body needs prose work, use humanizer on the body and come back here for the mechanics.

**The single most important rule:** rewriting history is destructive and cannot be undone once you have deleted the backups. Read "Scope before you touch" first, every time. A repo that is merely embarrassing is recoverable. A repo whose shared history you rewrote without asking is a Monday morning outage for everyone else who cloned it.

## What actually leaks

Ordered by how reliably it identifies a tool. The top group is a fingerprint. The bottom group is a guess.

### Tier 1: unambiguous

- **Commit trailers.** `Co-Authored-By: Claude <noreply@anthropic.com>`, `Co-Authored-By: Claude Opus 4.8 <noreply@anthropic.com>`, `Co-authored-by: Cursor Agent`, `Co-authored-by: Copilot`, `Co-authored-by: openai-codex[bot]`.
- **Generated-with footers** in commits, PR bodies, and issue bodies: `🤖 Generated with [Claude Code](https://claude.com/claude-code)`, `Generated with Cursor`, `Created by Devin`.
- **Bot authorship.** Check author *and* committer separately. `noreply@anthropic.com`, `cursoragent@cursor.com`, `*[bot]@users.noreply.github.com`, `devin-ai-integration[bot]`.
- **Branch names**: `claude/*`, `cursor/*`, `codex/*`, `devin/*`, `copilot/*`.
- **Assistant config committed by accident**: `.claude/`, `CLAUDE.md`, `.cursor/`, `.cursorrules`, `.aider*`, `.github/copilot-instructions.md`, `.windsurfrules`, `AGENTS.md`, `.specstory/`.
- **Attribution in docs**: "Built with Claude Code", "This project was generated by...".

### Tier 2: strong but circumstantial

- Em dashes in commit subjects. Almost nobody reaches for `—` in a terminal.
- Commit bodies with bolded inline headers, or `**Changes:**` followed by a bulleted rule of three.
- Subjects in Title Case, or subjects over ~72 chars that read like documentation.
- Emoji-prefixed subjects in a repo that has no gitmoji convention.
- A body that explains the *why* of something trivial at three-paragraph length.
- Suspiciously uniform commit cadence: forty commits in one hour, every message the same shape.

### Tier 2a: prose and content-level AI fingerprints

Based on the comprehensive [Wikipedia:Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing) field guide. These apply to any text in the repo, not just commit messages.

**Content signals**

- **Undue emphasis on significance/legacy** — phrases like *stands/serves as a*, *is a testament/reminder*, *a crucial/pivotal/vital/significant/key role*, *underscores/highlights its importance*, *reflects broader trends*, *setting the stage for*, *marking/shaping the*, *key turning point*, *evolving landscape*, *indelible mark*, *deeply rooted*. LLM writing puffs up even mundane subjects as if every etymology or census figure "highlights the enduring legacy" of something.
- **Canned notability claims** — *independent coverage*, *local/regional/national media outlets*, *trade publications*, *profiled in*, *written by a leading expert*, *active social media presence*. LLMs explicitly echo Wikipedia notability guidelines in the body text, e.g. "The subject has been profiled in multiple high-quality, independent, and widely-read outlets."
- **Superficial analyses** — present-participle (-ing) phrases tacked onto factual statements: *highlighting its significance*, *reflecting its importance*, *contributing to the*, *cultivating/fostering*, *enhancing its role*, *valuable insights*, *aligns/resonates with*. These are usually unattributed synthesis, not something the cited source actually says.
- **Promotional/ad language** — *boasts a*, *vibrant*, *rich*, *profound*, *showcasing*, *exemplifies*, *commitment to*, *nestled*, *in the heart of*, *groundbreaking*, *renowned*, *featuring*, *diverse array*. Even when told to write neutrally, LLMs drift into travel-guide or press-release tone. Older models (GPT-4) are more blatantly positive; newer ones are more subtly promotional but still overuse the same set of phrases.
- **Vague attributions** — *Industry reports*, *Observers have cited*, *Experts argue*, *Some critics argue*, *several sources/publications* (when only one or two are cited), *such as* (before exhaustive word lists). LLMs exaggerate how widely an opinion is held and present one or two sources as representing a consensus.
- **Outline-like "Challenges" sections** — rigid formula: "Despite its [positive words], [subject] faces several challenges..." followed by speculation about future potential. May also have a "Future Prospects" or "Challenges and Legacy" heading.
- **Leads treating list/article titles as proper nouns** — "Catchment area (health) refers to the geographic area..." or "The 'List of songs about Mexico' is a curated compilation..." — treating the article title as if it were a standalone entity.

**Language and grammar signals**

- **High density of "AI vocabulary"** — statistically documented overuse of words that spiked after 2022. Key terms:
  - 2023–mid 2024 (GPT-4): *additionally*, *boasts*, *bolstered*, *crucial*, *delve*, *emphasizing*, *enduring*, *garner*, *intricate/intricacies*, *interplay*, *key*, *landscape*, *meticulous*, *pivotal*, *underscore*, *tapestry*, *testament*, *valuable*, *vibrant*
  - Mid 2024–mid 2025 (GPT-4o): *align with*, *enhance*, *fostering*, *highlighting*, *showcasing*
  - Grok-specific: *causal*, *empirical*, *correlate*
  - Where there is one of these words, there are usually others nearby.
- **Avoidance of basic copulatives (is/are)** — replaces *is*/*are* with *serves as a*, *stands as*, *marks*, *functions as*, *represents a*, *boasts*, *features*, *offers*, *refers to*. Studies show a >10% drop in *is*/*are* usage in 2023 academic writing. Particularly visible in AI copyedits that "improve" text this way.
- **Negative parallelisms** — "Not just X, but also Y", "Not X, but Y", "X rather than Y". The text reads as if clearing up a misconception the reader might hold. Common in listicles but stereotypically AI when pervasive.
- **Rule of three** — overused structural pattern: "adjective, adjective, and adjective" or three-item bullet lists. LLMs use this to make superficial analyses appear more comprehensive.
- **Lexical diversity / elegant variation** — AI has a repetition-penalty that drives it to swap synonyms aggressively, making text feel like a thesaurus was applied. Causes noticeably varied word choice within a single paragraph that humans wouldn't naturally produce.

**Style signals**

- **Title case in section headings** — AI chatbots capitalize all main words in headings, unlike Wikipedia/standard sentence case convention.
- **Overuse of boldface** — mechanical bold for emphasis, often in a "key takeaways" fashion, inherited from readmes, fan wikis, and slide decks.
- **Inline-header vertical lists** — boldfaced header followed by colon, then descriptive text, often with non-standard list markers (•, -, #, emoji) instead of proper markup.
- **Overuse of em dashes** — used where humans would use commas, parentheses, or colons. Often surrounded by spaces (contrary to typographic convention). Some AIs (GPT-5.1) now suppress this because it became too well-known.
- **Emoji as formatting** — emoji decorating section headings or bullet points (more common in talk pages and edit summaries than article text).
- **Curly quotation marks and apostrophes** — ChatGPT and DeepSeek use curly quotes (“ ” ‘ ’) instead of straight ones. Can be inconsistent (mixing curly and straight in one response). Note: macOS/iOS and Word also produce curly quotes, so this alone is not decisive.
- **Skipping heading levels** — starting at `===` instead of `==` (level 3 instead of level 2), which breaks accessibility conventions.
- **Thematic breaks before headings** — `----` before each heading, a Markdown habit that leaks into wikitext.

**Markup and citation signals**

- **Use of Markdown instead of wikitext** — `**bold**` instead of `'''bold'''`, `# Heading` instead of `== Heading ==`, `[text](url)` instead of `[text url]`. Especially if inside fenced code blocks (```wikitext).
- **Broken wikitext** — garbled template syntax, especially around AfC submissions.
- **Internal reference markup bugs** — leaked AI citation code:
  - ChatGPT: `contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}`, `oai_citation:0`, `+1`, `turn0search0`, `turn0image0`, `{"attribution":{"attributableIndex":"X-Y"}}`
  - Gemini: `[cite: 1]`, `[span_1](start_span)`
  - Grok: `grok_card`, `grok_render_citation_card_json`
  - DeepSeek: lenticular brackets `「」`, dagger symbols `†`
  - Perplexity: `attached_file`, `ppl-ai-file-upload`
  - Unclassified: `:::writing`
- **Non-existent or out-of-place categories** — Wikipedia categories that don't exist or don't belong.
- **Broken external links, invalid DOI/ISBNs, DOIs leading to unrelated articles** — AI fabricates citations.
- **`utm_source=` parameters** in citations — a ChatGPT habit.
- **Named references declared in references section but unused in article body** — dead ref tags.

**Edit summary signals**

- **Canned assurance of adherence to Wikipedia policies** — "This edit ensures compliance with Wikipedia's guidelines on verifiability and neutrality," etc.
- **Specific mentions of "preserved" or "retained" information** — "Preserved the existing content while adding..."
- **Overemphasis on presence/reliability of citations** — "Added reliable sources," "supported by multiple independent citations."

**Communication-intended-for-user signals (leaked into article space)**

- **Collaborative phrases** — "I hope this helps!", "Of course!", "Certainly!", "Would you like...", "is there anything else", "let me know", "here is a...". Text addressed to the prompter that was pasted into the article.
- **Knowledge-cutoff disclaimers** — "As of my last knowledge update", "Up to my last training update", "While specific details are limited", "not widely documented/disclosed", "based on available information". In RAG models: speculation about gaps claiming info "likely" exists in a certain way.
- **Phrasal templates and placeholder text** — fill-in-the-blank brackets: `[Specific Topic]`, `[Link to source]`, `INSERT_SOURCE_URL_30`, `PASTE_YOUTUBE_URL_HERE`. Placeholder dates like `2025-XX-XX`. Comments saying "Add if available with citation."

### Tier 3: do not act on alone

- Well-written commit messages. Good engineers exist.
- Conventional Commits (`feat:`, `fix:`). That's a convention, not a tell.
- Curly quotes. Editors do that.
- A `Co-authored-by:` naming a **real human**. That's pair programming and stripping it steals credit. Check the name before you touch the line.

## Scope before you touch

Answer all five before running a single destructive command. Most of the risk lives here, not in the rewrite.

1. **What actually matches?** Don't guess.
   ```bash
   git log --all -i --grep="claude\|cursor\|copilot\|codex\|generated with" --oneline
   git log --all --format="%H %an <%ae> | %cn <%ce>" | grep -i "bot\|noreply@anthropic\|cursor"
   git log --all --format="%(trailers:key=Co-Authored-By)" | sort -u
   ```
2. **Is it pushed?** This is the hinge. A commit that never left the machine is free to rewrite.
   ```bash
   git branch -r --contains <sha>          # empty means local-only
   git rev-parse main origin/main          # do they differ?
   ```
   Check per-commit. Local `main` being ahead of `origin/main` is common, and it means those commits are yours alone even though they look "on main".
3. **Is it a shared branch?** Rewriting `main`/`master`/`develop` on a repo with other contributors is a different category of act. **Ask first, always.** Never fold it into a larger task.
4. **Are there open PRs on the branch?** A rewrite orphans the commits a PR references; the PR can end up empty or wildly confused.
5. **Are any commits GPG/SSH signed?** Rewriting **invalidates every signature** it touches. You cannot re-sign as the original author. If history is signed, say so and stop.

If the answer to 3 is yes, or 4/5 is yes, surface it and get an explicit decision. Do not proceed on the assumption that "strip the AI stuff" implies "and force-push my main".

## The rewrite

### Trailers only (the common case)

`filter-branch` is deprecated but ships with git; `git-filter-repo` is better if installed. Restrict the range so you don't touch history that doesn't need it.

```bash
# Back out point first. Cheap, and you will want it.
git tag backup-pre-declaude-main main
git tag backup-pre-declaude-branch <branch>

FILTER_BRANCH_SQUELCH_WARNING=1 git filter-branch -f \
  --msg-filter 'sed "/^Co-Authored-By: Claude/d; /^Co-authored-by: Cursor/d; /Generated with \[Claude Code\]/d"' \
  -- main <branch>
```

Prefer naming refs explicitly over `-- --all`. `--all` drags tags and every remote-tracking ref into the rewrite for no benefit.

**Trailing blank lines:** `sed` leaves the blank line that preceded the trailer. `git commit-tree` strips trailing blanks automatically, so this is usually fine, but check one message afterward rather than assuming.

### Also fixing authorship

A different filter, and note it must handle author and committer separately:

```bash
git filter-branch -f --env-filter '
if [ "$GIT_AUTHOR_EMAIL" = "noreply@anthropic.com" ]; then
  export GIT_AUTHOR_NAME="Real Name"
  export GIT_AUTHOR_EMAIL="real@example.com"
fi
if [ "$GIT_COMMITTER_EMAIL" = "noreply@anthropic.com" ]; then
  export GIT_COMMITTER_NAME="Real Name"
  export GIT_COMMITTER_EMAIL="real@example.com"
fi
' -- main <branch>
```

Get the replacement identity from the user or `git config user.name/user.email`. Never invent one.

### Purging a committed config directory

```bash
git filter-repo --invert-paths --path .claude --path CLAUDE.md   # preferred
# fallback:
git filter-branch -f --index-filter \
  'git rm -r --cached --ignore-unmatch .claude CLAUDE.md' -- --all
```
Then add it to `.gitignore`, or it walks straight back in on the next commit.

### Verify before you push

Two checks, not one. The second is the one people skip.

```bash
# 1. Is it gone?
git log --all --format="%H%n%B" | grep -i "claude\|co-authored" || echo clean

# 2. Did the merge-base survive with its SHA intact?
git rev-parse <last-good-sha>     # must still resolve
```

Check 2 matters more than it looks. filter-branch reprocesses every commit in range, but a commit whose message, tree, and parents are all unchanged **hashes to the same SHA**. So untouched ancestors keep their identity and `origin/main` doesn't diverge below the rewrite point. If that shared base *did* change SHA, your "small" branch rewrite just became a full history rewrite, and everyone else's clone is broken. Confirm it, don't hope.

### Expunge locally

The rewrite doesn't delete the old commits; it just stops referring to them. `filter-branch` deliberately keeps them in `refs/original/`, and the reflog holds them too. Until these are gone, nothing is stripped.

```bash
git tag -d backup-pre-declaude-main backup-pre-declaude-branch
git for-each-ref --format="%(refname)" refs/original | xargs -r -n1 git update-ref -d
git reflog expire --expire=now --all
git gc --prune=now
```

Only delete the backup tags once verification passed. That is the whole point of them.

### Push

```bash
git push --force-with-lease=refs/heads/<branch>:<old-sha> origin <branch>
```

Always `--force-with-lease` pinned to the SHA you expect, never bare `--force`. If someone pushed in the meantime, this refuses instead of destroying their work.

Only push refs the user asked you to push. If local `main` was rewritten but was never pushed, **leave it alone**. There is nothing to force and no reason to touch the remote.

## Tell the user what you could not fix

Non-negotiable. Do not report success without this, because "the commits are gone" is not actually true:

- **GitHub keeps orphaned commits server-side.** A force-push unreferences them; it does not delete them. The old SHA stays reachable at `github.com/<org>/<repo>/commit/<sha>` more or less indefinitely, and it is *also* recorded in the push event, the PR timeline, and any Actions run logs. Removing it for real requires GitHub Support garbage-collecting the repo, or deleting and re-creating the repo. Same story on GitLab and Bitbucket.
- **Forks and existing clones** keep the old history. You cannot reach them.
- **Anything already mirrored**: CI caches, artifact metadata, release assets, a `git log` pasted in a Slack thread.
- **Signatures** on rewritten commits are now invalid.

Say plainly which of these applies. If the goal was tidiness, they're done. If the goal was that nobody can *ever* retrieve it, they are not done and only the platform can finish the job.

## Beyond git

Same sweep, different surfaces:

- **PR/issue bodies**: `gh pr list --json number,body | grep -i "generated with"`, then `gh pr edit <n> --body ...`. Editing a body leaves an edit history visible to anyone who clicks "edited".
- **Code comments that narrate the diff** rather than describe the code. This is humanizer §30 and it is the single most common giveaway inside the source itself:
  > `// Changed from a loop to a map to fix the O(n²) issue`
  > → `// Hash map keeps lookup O(1); the naive scan was O(n²).`
  Related: comments restating the line below (`// increment counter`), and a comment density far above the rest of the file.
- **Commit-message prose**, via humanizer, with one important exception below.
- **Docs and README** attribution lines.

### The §30 exception

humanizer §30 says don't write diff-anchored prose. **Commit messages are the exception.** A commit is inherently version-scoped: describing what changed and why *is its job*. Do not "fix" a commit message for saying "this replaces the old X". Apply §30 to code comments and docs, never to commit bodies.

## Consent

Rewriting published history and force-pushing are irreversible and outward-facing.

- Local, unpushed commits: proceed.
- A pushed branch the user owns and just created: proceed if they asked for the strip, since the rewrite is meaningless without the push.
- A shared branch, default branch, or anything with an open PR: **stop and ask**, with the specific consequence spelled out.
- Approval to strip one branch is not approval to strip `main`.

Never quietly widen the blast radius because it seemed tidier.

## Output

Report:
1. What was found, by tier, with counts.
2. What changed, as an old-SHA → new-SHA table.
3. What was pushed, and what was deliberately left alone.
4. What survives anyway (the server-side section above). Do not bury this.
